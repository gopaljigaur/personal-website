Suppose nine training runs have reached their first checkpoint. Your policy is to continue the best third. Their validation losses are already available, and the ranking is unambiguous: A is best, then B, then C.

A worker becomes free. You send A back to training and remove it from the waiting queue. Another worker becomes free. You pick B. Then C.

What does the scheduler do when the fourth worker becomes free?

If it still compares all nine results, it starts a fresh trial. The best three have already been sent. If it compares only the six trials left in the queue, D now belongs to the top third. It gets more training too.

**A trial leaving the queue does not mean its result should leave the comparison.** That distinction changes how much work the scheduler allocates, even when no scores change and no new results arrive.

## The denominator is part of the algorithm

Early stopping makes a training budget go further by spending less on configurations that look unpromising. Successive halving formalizes this: train many configurations briefly, retain a fraction, and give the survivors a larger budget. A *rung* is one of those checkpoints.

In the promotion version of asynchronous successive halving, or ASHA, a free worker can resume an eligible paused trial without waiting for every trial to finish. [Algorithm 2 in the ASHA paper](https://arxiv.org/html/1810.05934v5) selects the top fraction of the results at a rung, then excludes candidates already promoted.

The two rules in the demonstration differ in which set they rank:

- **Rank first:** compare completed results, select the top fraction, then exclude trials already promoted.
- **Remove first:** exclude trials already promoted, then compare the remaining results and select their top fraction.

After three promotions in the example, rank-first still compares nine results and its three eligible trials have all been used. Remove-first compares six results. Its top two are D and E, and it sends D next. Five remain, so E becomes eligible. Then F. Then G. Only when two remain does the quota round down to zero.

For a fixed cohort of N completed trials, an integer reduction factor η, and distinct scalar losses, the difference can be written exactly:

To see the second expression, let p be the number already promoted. Another promotion remains possible whenever ⌊(N − p) / η⌋ is at least one. That continues through p = N − η, giving N − η + 1 promotions in total. If N is smaller than η, neither rule promotes anything.

For nine trials and η = 3, the counts are three and seven. As N grows with η fixed, the remove-first rule eventually promotes almost the entire cohort: its fraction is 1 − (η − 1) / N. The intended fixed-cohort fraction stays near 1 / η. This is a change in selection pressure, not a rounding detail.

## Why a hard cap is also wrong

It is tempting to fix the discrepancy by allowing at most one promotion for every three results. That works for the frozen example. An asynchronous run has another complication: evidence arrives over time, and earlier allocations cannot simply be taken back.

Start with three finished trials. A has the best loss, so it is promoted. Now a fourth trial, D, finishes with a lower loss than A. The current top third contains D. Blocking D because a promotion has already happened would ignore the new evidence.

<ArrivalExplorer />

If A is promoted before D finishes, two of the four trials can legitimately receive more training. If D finishes first, only D is promoted. The scores are identical; the decision history differs.

The frozen-cohort test and the late-arrival test therefore check different properties. With no new evidence, repeatedly requesting work should not steadily lower the qualification threshold. With new evidence, a newly qualified trial must still be able to advance. Counting promotions alone cannot tell those cases apart.

This also explains why pending trials need their own state. A running trial occupies a worker, but it has not yet supplied a result for a ranking. Treating a missing score as another member of the completed cohort can enlarge the quota before there is evidence to justify it.

## Where this showed up

This distinction came up while examining [NePS's asynchronous bracket code at revision d054d482](https://github.com/automl/neps/blob/d054d482c3ea5d184111fe47b664b4508aaaab07/neps/optimizers/utils/brackets.py). Its selection logic removes previous promotions before ranking. In a controlled scheduler-only reproduction, nine completed trials produced allocations of **9 → 7 → 5** across budgets of 1, 3, and 9. A local rank-first patch produced **9 → 3 → 1**.

There is relevant history: [issue #231](https://github.com/automl/neps/issues/231) argued for removing promotions before selection, and [PR #232](https://github.com/automl/neps/pull/232) implemented it. The current behavior was deliberate. The question for maintainers is whether that policy is intended, despite differing from the paper's selection rule. The local patch has not been submitted or reviewed upstream.

This is not evidence that other ASHA implementations have the same problem. [Syne Tune explicitly distinguishes stopping and promotion variants](https://syne-tune.readthedocs.io/en/latest/tutorials/basics/basics_asha.html). A stopping scheduler decides whether an arriving trial should continue; a promotion scheduler chooses which paused trial to resume. [Ray Tune's implementation](https://docs.ray.io/en/latest/_modules/ray/tune/schedulers/async_hyperband.html) uses a percentile-based stopping decision. Its startup behavior, thresholds, and event ordering must be evaluated on their own terms. The exact counts above cannot be applied to it.

Nor do extra promotions establish worse final model quality. They allocate more work to the existing cohort, potentially leaving less budget for new configurations. Measuring the resulting trade-off requires real learning curves under a matched total budget. The reproduction establishes the scheduling difference; it does not establish a training speedup or an accuracy improvement.

The practical check is small enough to run without a GPU: freeze a rung's results and keep asking for work. Then let one better result arrive and ask again. A scheduler should be able to explain both decisions using the scores it has seen, the trials it has already promoted, and the policy it claims to implement.

The Python reproduction loads the actual NePS scheduler modules. It begins with distinct scalar losses and completes promoted trials immediately. The browser demonstrations are separate teaching models of a single rung; they do not execute NePS.

The local patch filters pending rows, ranks completed results, then excludes earlier promotions. It passes 25 focused checks, including asynchronous completion traces compared against an independent scalar reference. The original focused suite had ten failures and five passes before the patch. These are module-level checks, not full-runtime or distributed-training validation. Multi-objective checks cover strictly dominated chains, not arbitrary nondominated fronts.

The investigation, patch, and interactive explanation were developed with AI assistance. The experiment code remains outside this website repository. This draft documents an implementation finding and a known algorithm; it does not introduce a new optimization method.
